Introduction Implantable brain–computer interfaces (iBCIs) hold great potential to replace lost or impaired functions of the body for individuals with severe paralysis. After decades of research in laboratory settings, iBCIs are now progressing toward real-world use, supported by ongoing clinical trials. There is a need for patient usability both during and after clinical trials, where a research team may no longer be available to support users. To function autonomously, means to act independently, without external influence or control. The concept of autonomy extends even beyond independent function and includes self-governance over one’s iBCI use, free from privacy concerns or restrictions. Traditional measures of BCI performance may not necessarily include measures of autonomous function, focusing rather on the ceiling of performance in a controlled environment. Measures of iBCI autonomy may be crucial for patient adoption beyond highly supported clinical trials. Here, we present four key considerations to achieve autonomous use, reflecting the authors’ perspectives based on discussions during various sessions and workshops across the 10th International BCI Society Meeting, Brussels, 2023: (1) immediate use, (2) easy to use, (3) continuous use, and (4) stable system use. 1.1. Immediate use For successful adoption of iBCIs in commercial settings, the time from device implantation or activation to effective use should be minimal. Studies of assistive technologies consistently report that setup time is a key predictor of real-world use and user satisfaction [1–3]. Ideally, marketed iBCI systems would offer seamless integration and instant functionality, i.e. a ‘plug-and-play’ solution. Historically, calibration tasks typically lasted over 30 min [1, 4, 5] and required hundreds of task repetitions to collect data [6]. The growth of deep learning and its applications to iBCI [6–10] also increased the demand for training data, with some applications requiring multiple days, weeks, or even months of calibration data [6–8, 11]. This imposes a significant burden on patients with severe motor impairments and their care partners, where caregiver burden is already high [12]. Without the support of a research team, such time commitments to training iBCI models may be impractical. Encouragingly, recent advances in iBCI calibration techniques have successfully reduced training times and the amount of calibration data needed for effective use. Rapid online calibration techniques that reduce both time and data requirements are being developed [13–16], with the ultimate goal being effective use without prior active calibration [17]. Equally important is the need to plan for real-world use in the post-market setting, including the necessary infrastructure to support initial iBCI training (onboarding). Support from company representatives and allied health professionals such as occupational therapists or speech-language pathologists may be essential. However, to be scalable: (1) initial training must be sufficiently concise for a relatively small team of company representatives to support a large number of patients, and (2) initial training should be both sufficiently brief and immediately focused on achieving occupational goals to gain coverage by payors such as Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services or private insurance companies in the United States [18]. In addition, appropriately qualified clinical personnel should require minimal supplemental training to support iBCI onboarding. 1.2. Easy to use To achieve adoption, iBCIs should be easy for both patients and their care partners to learn and use. Accordingly, iBCI systems should require minimal mental and physical effort from patients, while minimal setup requirements may be essential for patient and care partner adherence. 1.2.1. Low mental and physical effort iBCI systems should be intuitive and easy to use, requiring minimal mental and physical effort for patients to understand and operate. For example, attempted or imagined movements used to initiate iBCI actions should not demand a level of mental effort that limits the iBCI’s intended duration of use. Non-intuitive control strategies, for example, controlling a robotic arm via decoded attempted foot movements, may increase effortfulness. In contrast, leveraging natural attempted or imagined movements, such as handwriting movements for handwriting-to-text generation [7] or speech articulator movements to facilitate speech-to-text generation [8, 11], can help users overcome steep learning curves. Reducing mental and physical demands during iBCI use may improve performance and adoption rates. Studies of assistive technology suggest that users are more likely to abandon devices that are difficult to learn or use [19, 20]. For example, while eye-tracking assistive technology is widely used, it sometimes requires a high mental and physical effort from users, leading to strain, fatigue, and device abandonment [21, 22]. Similarly, in a recent iBCI study, a participant with dysarthria reported that attempting voiced speech for speech neuroprosthesis control was fatiguing due to limited breath control [11]. However, this and other recent studies have demonstrated the possibility of decoding covert or silent speech [8, 11, 23], which might improve ease of use for some patients. Tools such as animations, physical demonstrations of control behaviors, and movement imagery might also facilitate iBCI training and improve performance [24, 25]. However, iBCI researchers and developers should be aware that the movements preferred by users for iBCI control are not always the movements that are expected [24]. Adopting a user-centered design approach [3, 24, 26] can guide the development of iBCI systems that are more user-friendly and require minimal mental effort. 1.2.2. Low setup requirements In addition to patients, iBCI users include care partners who assist with iBCI system setup and cleanup in the absence of a research team. Some of the first iBCIs to obtain regulatory approval may be indicated for individuals with severe paralysis, where a high burden on care partners exists [12]. Therefore, setup procedures must be both quick and simple to accommodate busy and sometimes stressful daily routines. Studies indicate that care partner burden and environmental limitations have been barriers to BCI adoption [21]. The time and effort needed for setup are also strong factors predicting real-world BCI use [1, 27]. Simplicity of setup and operation also includes low requirement for training on the iBCI system. Ideally, commercial iBCIs would not require specialized skillsets, as this could create dependency on individually trained care partners and increase the risk of burden or discontinued use. This contrasts with the highly specialized interdisciplinary teams that typically supervise iBCI use during academic research studies [28]. While ease of use is an obvious requirement for any medical device intended for at-home use, this is a critical factor in post-market success for iBCI. iBCI training and support adds cost and organizational effort for user support, posing an additional challenge to payor coverage. iBCIs must be sufficiently easy to use with minimal support from allied health professionals in the post-visit period. 1.3. Continuous use As identified at the 10th International BCI Society Meeting [29], a potential clinical benefit of iBCIs is the provision of functional independence and autonomy. In consideration of autonomy, iBCIs should ideally offer continuous functionality when and where it is needed by the patient. While some iBCI devices may not be required throughout the day, the ability to access them as needed may be both clinically significant and essential for daily adoption. Continuous digital autonomy has been identified as an unmet clinical need for individuals with severe paralysis [30]. With the proliferation of digital technologies, maintaining social relationships, alerting a care partner to medical issues, and participating in daily activities may all rely on continuous digital access for individuals unable to move their bodies. As such, quality of life and various clinical outcomes may be significantly impacted by the ability to communicate with others and the environment via on demand availability of digital technology [24, 31, 32]. Reliance on care partners for system setup and adjustments has also been found to negatively impact feelings of independence [33]. Current augmentative and assistive technologies such as sip-and-puff controllers [34], eye tracking systems [35], and head movement-controlled systems [36] offer some autonomy to people with paralysis but can also require repeated repositioning or recalibrating by a care partner. Consequently, the utility of some of these technologies is predominantly confined to controlled and closed environments, such as the home, with constant care partner support. Many assistive technology companies advise against using these devices during travel due to potential malfunctions from motion or environmental factors (e.g. glare) or the risk of injury should the device fall on the user. In this regard, fully implantable and wireless iBCI systems offer the benefit of being integrated within the body, thereby minimizing the need for frequent alignment or repositioning that is common with conventional assistive technology. Meeting the need for continuous digital autonomy requires a technological interface that is continuously ‘on’ and available without the need for assistance, regardless of environment or time of day. One specific challenge for iBCI technologies for users with severe paralysis is initiating communication without care partner assistance, particularly during overnight periods when uninterrupted digital access is crucial for communicating healthcare needs; a scenario in which conventional augmentative and alternative communication technologies can often fail. This challenge is compounded by neural signals underlying iBCI control differing between sleep and awake states [37], non-task-elicited brain activity generating iBCI classification errors [37] or ‘false alarms’ [38], and visuomotor skill acquisition (often performed for iBCI calibration) involving replay of corresponding neural activity during sleep [39]. Encouragingly, recent work has exemplified the potential for continuous digital access via iBCI by demonstrating >20 h of daily use of an iBCI by a person with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, including overnight use [37, 40]. 1.4. Stable system use Maintaining iBCI performance often requires frequent decoder recalibration to counteract changes in neural recordings over time [41], which has typically involved supervised data collection performed by a research team. Frequent decoder recalibration may also impede the consolidation of control strategies and lead to performance variability across days [15, 33]. For iBCI performance to remain stable, it must withstand changes and drifts in measured signal features, which can occur due to various factors such as device migration [42], degradation [43], the influence of varying behavior [44, 45], and physiological changes in neuronal activity [41, 46, 47]. These drifts can occur over hours to days [41, 48]. While decoder stability remains a challenge for some iBCI systems, a recent study has demonstrated highly accurate real-time neural decoding without model retraining or recalibration over multiple months [23]. Another approach to reducing the need for frequent recalibration is to continually update the underlying decoder using techniques such as decoder adaptation [15, 49], which typically involves users performing tasks or providing feedback about decoding accuracy. Alternatively, online recalibration might enable a self-recalibrating system with automatic decoder updates that run in the background [14]. Robust decoders and automated recalibration techniques will be essential in the commercial setting, where there is no dedicated research team and potentially large numbers of patients to support. iBCI hardware will also have to facilitate long-term reliability with minimal signal loss. External components categorized as durable medical equipment may be eligible for replacement or upgrading at least once every five years [50]. To replace implanted components, patients would need to undergo surgery, thus these components should ideally provide at least ten years of use. However, chronic implants that directly interface with the brain might be expected to experience a decline in recording capabilities over time [51]. Improvements in materials and design are expected to improve longevity of implants that directly interface with the brain. Other implanted components may be permanent without the possibility of explant [52]. 2. Discussion We have presented four requirements of autonomous BCI use that we believe are necessary for the successful clinical translation of iBCIs. These requirements reflect the authors’ perspective on recurring themes discussed amongst attendees at the 10th International BCI Society Meeting. We recommend these requirements remain under the consideration of groups developing iBCIs and demonstration of these requirements be included in pivotal trials conducted in support of market approval. We acknowledge these proposals overlap with a large field of user experience research that various BCI groups have both contributed to and considered. Studies investigating BCI usability and effectiveness over years of use have previously emphasized the importance of requirements such as minimal setup assistance for both satisfaction and sustained use [53, 54]. User experience factors including usability, affect, ergonomics, and quality of life will also significantly impact the real-world acceptance and use of iBCI technology [53, 55]. Moreover, individual needs and priorities of various user groups including those with severe motor impairments due to stroke [24, 56–58], spinal cord injury [3, 19, 24, 56, 59, 60], and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [2, 24, 56, 60–62], as well as care partners [56, 61–63], have been identified, with the goal of successfully integrating BCI into existing physical and social contexts. Several other important considerations must also be addressed for the clinical translation of iBCIs. Safety remains top priority. Several iBCIs currently under development either require implantation through craniotomy [64] or endovascular procedures [52], and early evidence suggests a favorable safety profile with few serious device-related adverse events. This is consistent with other chronically implanted neurotechnologies such as deep brain stimulators [65]. Additionally, ethical concerns related to privacy, agency, and social acceptance must remain under consideration [66, 67]. The successful adoption of iBCIs will depend on effectively educating patients and caregivers to foster awareness of the technology’s capabilities and set realistic expectations for its benefits. Building health professionals’ understanding of iBCIs and optimizing care pathways will ensure appropriate patient selection and access to future iBCIs. Deliberately, we have mostly remained agnostic to the intended function of iBCIs, which will need to be meaningful and correspond to an unmet clinical need. Further discussions are necessary to assign different requirements to specific use cases. We believe that these four requirements will be crucial for several iBCI applications for users with severe paralysis but recognize they may be less essential for users with less severe disability who may not depend on the iBCI for their daily needs and social well-being. Finally, we acknowledge that these requirements are not exhaustive and encourage further dialogue within the iBCI community.
